### Footnotes Protocol for Drafting (by GPT)

# by GPT

# 1. When to add a footnote

A note exists only if it does at least one of these:

**Clarify evidence**

identify an instrument, place, or technical term

explain how Harrow knows something

**Connect documents**

link one artifact to another (e.g., Cylinder 9 ↔ diary entry)

**Reveal interpretation**

show how Harrow reads the event or phenomenon

**Expose uncertainty**

acknowledge damaged text, degraded recording, missing context

If a note only **repeats the sentence**, **summarizes the obvious**, or **adds color**, it should not exist.

Rule of thumb:

If removing the footnote changes nothing in the reader’s understanding, delete it.

# 2. How many notes per document

Keep them sparse. The archive should breathe.

Typical ranges:

Document typeNotesScientific record2–4Personal diary1–3Memorandum1–2Meeting minutes2–4Letters1–2Fragments0–2

More than **four** usually means the editor is overexplaining.

# 3. What Harrow’s notes do

Harrow is the **discoverer of the archive** and the one replaying the artifacts.

His notes tend to:

describe **physical properties** of objects

explain **instruments or measurements**

connect **multiple artifacts**

speculate about **anomalies**

document **his own examination**

Tone characteristics:

observational

slightly fascinated

increasingly drawn toward the phenomenon

not fully objective

He sometimes **leans toward the uncanny**.

Example style:

The pitch described here corresponds closely to the tone recovered from Cylinder 9 during playback.

# 4. What Ellis’s notes do

Ellis appears only when needed.

He does **three things only**:

**Verify**

confirm authenticity

describe modern tests

**Limit Harrow**

note when Harrow’s interpretation is speculative

**Clarify provenance**

explain how documents were recovered or transmitted

Ellis is cooler, skeptical, professional.

Example style:

Replication tests detect no persistent magnetic field in the document.

# 5. Who gets the first note

Order always works like this:

Harrow note

Ellis comment (only if needed)

Ellis never appears **alone** unless the note concerns **provenance or archival handling**.

# 6. What not to do

No notes that:

summarize the text

narrate what the reader just read

invent supporting evidence

explain obvious emotions

restate measurements already in the line

clutter the form line without reason

The document itself must carry the story.

# 7. Where notes attach

Attach them to:

**technical claims**

**unusual phenomena**

**historical context**

**interpretive shifts**

Avoid attaching them to:

ordinary description

emotional statements

narrative flow unless needed

# 8. Form lines

Form lines describe **the artifact**, not the story.

Typical elements:

physical material

handwriting or medium

condition

where it was found

who preserved it

Never interpret motives in the form line.

# 9. Narrative function of footnotes

The notes quietly create a **three-stage drama**:

**1856:** witnesses struggle to understand events

**2010:** Harrow begins to see patterns

**2023:** Ellis reins him in

The reader experiences the mystery through that tension.

A good way to think about it is this: **footnotes are not explanations — they are narrative instruments**. If they don’t move the archive’s story forward in some way, they shouldn’t exist.

So the question isn’t “can we add a note here?” but **“what narrative work would this note perform?”**

Below is the practical framework that keeps them disciplined.

# 1. A footnote must change the reader’s understanding

A note should **add a new layer of meaning**, not restate the line.

If the reader’s understanding is identical before and after the note, the note fails.

Example:

Bad

“v.p.s. means vibrations per second.”

Good

This frequency matches the tone recovered from Cylinder 9.

The second **connects documents** and advances the story.

# 2. Footnotes exist when two times collide

The archive has two time periods:

**1856 – the event**

**2010 / 2023 – the investigation**

A note is justified when the **later investigators can see something the original writers could not**.

Examples:

• Harrow replaying the cylinder

• Ellis testing paper or ink

• modern comparison of frequencies

• reconstruction of sequence

This creates the narrative effect of **discovery across time**.

# 3. A note should do at least one of four narrative jobs

Every note should serve **one of these functions**.

### A. Connect artifacts

The archive becomes a web.

Example

Diary ↔ cylinder

Logbook ↔ meeting minutes

Letter ↔ later testimony

These connections are extremely valuable because they **build the investigative structure of the book**.

### B. Reveal interpretation

Harrow’s notes often show **how the meaning of the event evolves**.

Example

Rooke: mechanical anomaly

Selina: divine order

Harrow: pattern emerging

This deepens the narrative.

### C. Mark uncertainty

Archives feel real when they acknowledge limits.

Examples

damaged page

unclear audio

disputed authorship

lost instrument

Ellis especially performs this role.

### D. Introduce anomaly

Some notes quietly highlight that **something should not be happening**.

Examples

persistent frequency

strange magnetic reading

recurring phrase

identical measurement in separate sources

This builds the supernatural tension without overt exposition.

# 4. Do NOT add notes just because you can

Avoid notes that:

• repeat the sentence

• define obvious terms

• summarize the paragraph

• explain emotions

• narrate what already happened

Those make the archive feel fake.

Real archives have **selective commentary**.

# 5. Footnotes should be rare at moments of high drama

When the document itself is intense, let it stand.

Example:

Amara panic entry

Rooke protest

Selina prayer

Adding many notes there breaks immersion.

Use notes mostly where the document is **technical, ambiguous, or revealing**.

# 6. The three editorial personalities shape the story

Footnotes are part of character development.

### Harrow

curious

interpretive

increasingly obsessed

notices patterns everywhere

His notes gradually **lean toward belief**.

### Ellis

restrained

skeptical

corrects Harrow

verifies provenance

His notes prevent the archive from becoming mystical nonsense.

### The reader

The reader sits **between Harrow and Ellis**, deciding who to trust.

That tension is a major narrative engine.

# 7. A useful litmus test

Before adding a note, ask:

**Does this note create a connection, reveal doubt, or deepen the mystery?**

If the answer is **no**, delete it.

# 8. The ideal note density

For a document-heavy narrative like this:

Average **1–3 notes per artifact**.

Scientific documents can go slightly higher.

Diaries usually go lower.

# 9. What the footnotes ultimately do for the story

They transform the archive from:

**a stack of old papers**

into

**an investigation unfolding across centuries**.

Without them, the documents are static.

With them, the reader watches the mystery **being rediscovered**.

# The Footnote Decision Tree

Before writing a note, run the line through these checks **in order**.

If the answer is **no** at every step, **do not add a footnote**.

# Step 1 — Does the reader need context that the 1856 writer could not know?

This is the most common justification.

Examples

• modern playback of a cylinder

• spectral analysis

• material testing

• reconstruction of sequence

If yes → **Harrow footnote**

Example

Rooke mentions a frequency → Harrow later confirms it matches the cylinder recording.

# Step 2 — Does this line connect to another document in the archive?

If the note creates a **cross-link**, it strengthens the investigative structure.

Examples

Selina diary ↔ Cylinder 9

Finch journal ↔ Rooke log

Meeting minutes ↔ later experiment

If yes → **Harrow footnote**

# Step 3 — Is there something physically unusual about the artifact itself?

Sometimes the object tells part of the story.

Examples

• heat damage

• magnetization

• wax deformation

• graphite scoring

• acoustic trace in paper

If yes → **Harrow footnote**

# Step 4 — Is Harrow interpreting something the original writers misunderstood?

This is where Harrow’s growing obsession enters.

Examples

Rooke thinks vibration is mechanical

Harrow suspects resonance pattern

Selina thinks the field is divine

Harrow notes frequency matches apparatus output

If yes → **Harrow footnote**

# Step 5 — Does Harrow go too far?

If Harrow’s note becomes speculative, Ellis may intervene.

Use Ellis **sparingly**.

Ellis notes should do one of three things:

• confirm provenance

• limit Harrow’s speculation

• correct a measurable claim

Example

Harrow claims magnetic anomaly.

Ellis notes replication shows none.

# Step 6 — Would the note reveal something eerie or unexpected?

Some notes quietly deepen the mystery.

Examples

• identical frequency appearing across documents

• a phrase that affects playback

• resonance appearing in unrelated objects

These are some of the **most powerful notes** in the archive.

# Step 7 — Would removing the note change the reader’s understanding?

Final test.

If removing the note **changes nothing**, delete it.

# Placement rule

Attach the note to the **most specific phrase** that triggers it.

Not the whole paragraph.

Example

Bad placement

Footnote after entire paragraph.

Good placement

Footnote after “1 985 v.p.s.”

# Density rule

Use roughly:

**1–3 notes per document**

Scientific records may have **3–4**.

Personal writing often has **1–2**.

More than four means the archive voice is intruding too much.

# The hidden narrative effect

When used correctly, footnotes gradually show:

1856 → witnesses confused

2010 → Harrow reconstructing the phenomenon

2023 → Ellis questioning Harrow

The reader watches the **meaning of the experiment evolve across time**.
