## The Aetheric Engine (Visual Novel / Isometric Layer)

## Overview

Richmond House is a mid-Victorian country manor in Surrey, leased by Selina Deveraux for the duration of the experiment. It is neither grand nor modest — a working house with high-ceilinged principal rooms and an attached coach-house. By September 1856, after the final experiment, the house is partially degraded: softened floorboards, tarnished brass, ozone smell in the hall runner. The machine still stands in the Gallery.

In the isometric game layer, Rowan navigates a **living reconstruction** of the house as it existed during the active experiment period (summer-autumn 1856), before the final catastrophe. Characters are present and going about their lives. The degradation effects (softened wood, tarnished brass, thin air) are environmental texture, not yet at their worst.

## Atmospheric Notes for Art Direction

- **Lighting:** Afternoon light, late summer. Long shadows. Gas lamps not yet lit. The Gallery is the brightest room (south-facing windows). Selina's Study is dim by design — she keeps the curtains partially drawn.
- **Sound design:** The Gallery has a persistent low harmonic hum — barely audible. In the upper corridor, the floorboards creak differently than expected. The conservatory is quieter than it should be.
- **Ningal's presence:** Not a character walking the rooms. Instead: objects slightly out of place, a lamp flame that doesn't flicker in the breeze, cold air near the east wall of the Gallery where the field is strongest. The player may not identify this as Ningal until late in the game.
- **Scale:** Isometric grid. The Gallery should feel large — it needs to contain the machine and allow Rowan to circle it. The Workshop is cramped. The Drawing Room is formally proportioned. Selina's Study is smaller than expected for the mistress of the house.

## Victorian Realism Notes

### Bathrooms — there aren't any

1856 is early for indoor plumbing in private homes. Richmond House would **not** have a bathroom in any recognizable modern sense.

- **Bathing:** Done in a portable tin bath brought to the bedroom and filled by servants carrying hot water from the kitchen. Each resident would bathe in their own room. The servants carry water up and carry the bath back down.
- **Toileting:** A small **earth closet** or very early **water closet** (a seat above a tank, emptied manually) may exist somewhere off the back corridor or in an outbuilding. Characters would not discuss this. Chamber pots remain in the bedrooms overnight.
- **Game implication:** No bathroom to explore. If a player wonders where everyone relieves themselves, they're asking the right kind of uncomfortable historical question — and the answer is: not conveniently, and with significant servant labor.

### Closets — also mostly absent

Victorian houses did not have built-in wardrobes or walk-in closets. Storage was freestanding:

- **Bedrooms:** Each bedroom has an armoire or wardrobe (freestanding furniture). These could be interactive objects but are not separate rooms.
- **Upper landing:** The **linen press** (included in the floor plan) is the closest thing to a closet — a shallow cupboard for folded sheets, towels, nightclothes. It exists but is not inhabited.
- **Under the stairs:** A small under-stair storage area, probably locked. Boots, umbrellas, items waiting to be moved.

### On the servants

Clara Keene and her mother are the confirmed domestic staff. A house this size running an active experiment would need at minimum: cook/housekeeper (Clara's mother), housemaid (Clara), and possibly a manservant for heavier work. The novel focuses on Clara as the observing voice. Her mother is present but unnamed. No other servants are individuated.

**For the game:** Clara and her mother are real presences in the kitchen and servants' hall. Clara is a potential character to interact with — she notices everything and trusts none of Bell's grand theories.
